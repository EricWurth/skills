# Cover Letter Guide

*Structure: Problem-Solution. Built from hiring-manager survey research (2026
vintage — numbers below are directional, refresh if they start driving decisions).
Company research is required, not optional.*

---

## When to build one

Ad-hoc, not automatic. Build only when the portal has a cover-letter field at
apply time, or when you decide a target warrants it.

Worth knowing when deciding: ~45% of hiring managers read the letter *before*
the resume, and ~73% read one even when the company doesn't require it. Large
employers and the healthcare, finance, law, government, and nonprofit sectors
weight letters highest. But ~18% of hiring managers say a weak letter has
eliminated an otherwise-strong candidate — **a rushed letter is worse than none.**

---

## Non-negotiables

- Always a **separate file**, never page two of the resume. Merging breaks ATS
  parsing, and portals have distinct upload fields.
- **Export PDF for submission**, keep the .docx as source.
- **300–400 words.** One page, well under half a page of body text. ~70% of
  employers prefer half a page or less; ~36% of readers spend under 30 seconds.
- **Company research is required.** ~90% of rejected cover letters fail for lack
  of customization — not prose quality. A letter tailored only to the job
  description still reads as generic.

## Structure

### Header
Same contact block as your resume, so the packet reads as a set. Then the date,
then the salutation. Use a named person if you can find one; otherwise
`Dear [Company] hiring team,` — never "To Whom It May Concern," which now
actively hurts.

### Paragraph 1 — The problem (60–80 words)
Open on a specific challenge *this employer* has, sourced from real research: a
recent announcement, a regulatory pressure, a named initiative, or what the
posting reveals about why the role exists. Then one sentence connecting it to
what you do. Name the role somewhere in this paragraph, but not as the opening
clause. This paragraph carries the most weight — ~41% of hiring managers name
the introduction as the most impressive element, and a third of readers never
get much past it.

### Paragraph 2 — The proof (100–120 words)
The closest analogous thing you have actually done, told as ONE story with
specifics — not a list. One engagement described properly beats three resume
bullets restated in prose. Expand on something the resume can only gesture at:
the situation, what you did, what changed.

### Paragraph 3 — The second angle (80–100 words)
Either sector credibility (regulated-industry experience, domain frameworks that
map to their world) or the second capability the role needs. **Cut this
paragraph entirely if it would be filler** — three tight paragraphs beat four
padded ones.

### Paragraph 4 — Close (~40 words)
Forward-looking and specific to them. One clear ask. Mention attachments only if
recommendation letters are actually going.

---

## Do not

- Open with "I'm writing to apply for" or "I am writing to express my interest
  in" — the single most-flagged generic opener. (~74% of hiring managers say
  they can spot AI-written applications; ~80% of those view it negatively.)
- Restate resume bullets. Expand or connect instead.
- Use em dashes or bold anything mid-sentence — both are AI tells and banned by
  your content rules.
- Write anything that could apply to another company without editing.
- Use aphorisms or rhetorical flourishes. Plain, concise, optimistic. Match your
  register, don't upgrade it.
- Explain why you left, apologize for a gap, or pre-concede anything. Gaps get
  addressed ONLY when the posting marks the qualification "required" and the
  reviewer will certainly notice — then exactly one sentence: plain fact +
  experience anchor + confident willingness to close it. Never for "preferred"
  qualifications, never a "note on..." paragraph, never apologetic.
- Mention salary unless the posting explicitly asks.
- Close weakly. There should be a clear ask.

## Research checklist (before writing)

1. **What they are actually dealing with:** recent news, a funding round, an
   acquisition, a regulatory action, a public initiative. One concrete thing.
2. **Why the role exists:** read past the requirements list — most postings
   reveal the problem somewhere (a program being rebuilt, a function being stood
   up, a capability gap).
3. **A name:** the hiring manager or function head, from the posting or LinkedIn.
4. **Sector-specific pressure:** what regulatory or market context are they in,
   and which of your real experience maps to it.

If you cannot find anything specific after a reasonable look, the letter will
read generic — reconsider whether it is worth sending.

---

## Attachments

Recommendation letters (in `Reference/`) are ad-hoc too. If you keep several and
the portal caps uploads, decide your selection order once — which letter leads
for which role family — and record it here so every apply session picks the same
way. Uncapped portals can take resume + letter + all recommendations.

---

## Production

Generate the .docx with the plugin's builder so the letter matches the resume's
visual identity, lint it, QA it (claims trace to the master; letter matches THIS
posting), convert to PDF, and file as:

```
Applications/{Company}_{Role}/{YourName}_{Company}_{Role}_CoverLetter.pdf
```
